Who uses it, and how

Most of Europe's smaller languages, from Estonian to Maltese, rarely get a dub. The same app serves a weekly fansub release, a classroom project and a parent making a cartoon their child can follow. Here are eight groups, what they set up, and the screens they spend their time on. A ninth dubs games, with FanSUB's sister app GameDUB.

A fansub group adds a dub to its weekly release

A Latvian or Slovenian fansub group already translates every episode. Now its members also voice the characters, each from home, and the release ships with a dubbed audio track next to the original.

Host: the group's encoder, on a MacCast: 4–8 members, any browser
01
  1. Prepare on release dayThe host imports the MKV with the group's own subtitle, lets FanSUB separate dialogue from music and assigns each line to a character.
  2. Open a sessionOne link, QR code and password go into the group chat. Members pick their character; taken ones show who has them.
  3. Record during the weekEach actor gets only their lines, the clip, the original voice as a guide and the background. Takes are sent when they're happy.
  4. Approve and releaseThe host is told as each take comes in, and the Dashboard shows which lines are still missing. Approve takes or ask for a retake with a note, then exports the MKV with the dub as the default audio and the original kept.
Why it works for a weekly schedule: characters, voice actors and voices carry over from one episode to the next, so episode two starts where episode one ended.

A team with no voice actors dubs the whole episode with local AI

One or two people want a dub in Maltese or Lithuanian, but have nobody to record. Each character gets a voice, the Mac speaks every line, and the team spends its time listening and fixing instead of recording.

One MacOmniVoice model, 1.4 GB
02
  1. Give each character a voiceClone it from a clean passage of the episode, pick a section by hand, import a recording, or design one from a description.
  2. Generate voicesChoose the characters and Best or Faster. Each line is spoken in the target language and fitted to the time the original took. A 160-line episode takes about 6–11 minutes.
  3. Fix what doesn't fitLines that are too long are listed. Give one a shorter alternative or more time, and generate only that line again.
  4. Check and approveGenerated takes are listened to again: wrong words or the wrong voice are flagged. Approve the rest together, then watch it all in the Theatre.
Nothing leaves the Mac. The voice model runs locally: no account, no upload, no per-minute bill. Its licence allows non-commercial use only.

A half-full cast: people for the leads, AI for everyone else

The group has voices for the three main characters but not for the shopkeeper, the teacher and the crowd. People record the leads; the side characters are generated and come into the same inbox.

Lead actors in browsersSide characters generated
03
  1. Split the castLeads are picked in the fandub session as usual. In Generate voices, tick only the characters nobody is voicing.
  2. Keep the human takes"Keep lines that already have a recorded take" stays on, so a generated line never replaces a person's recording.
  3. Use a real voice for the AIA group member who doesn't want to act can still lend a voice: import their recording and a side character speaks with it.
  4. One inbox for bothRecorded and generated takes are approved, edited or regenerated in the same Takes inbox, one take per line.
Swap later: when a new member joins, they pick the character and record. Their approved take wins over the generated one.

A voice-acting community casts across towns and countries

Estonian actors live in Tallinn, Tartu and abroad, and record at different hours. The host runs the session behind a reverse proxy so everyone can reach it, and gives notes per line instead of directing in one room.

Host with a domain or tunnelActors anywhere, on any device
04
  1. Reach people outside the home networkPut the session behind Caddy, nginx or a tunnel. The app shows the address to forward to and puts the public link in the QR code.
  2. Record on the goThe booth works on a phone. Actors can keep their lines for offline practice, with the guide voice and the background stored in their browser.
  3. Direct with notesAsk for a retake with a note on a single line; the actor sees it next to that line in their booth.
  4. Credit everyoneThe name an actor types when joining goes into the voice credits of the release.

A class dubs a short film as a language project

A teacher in Ljubljana or Bratislava runs the session on one Mac in the classroom. Pupils join from school laptops or their phones, each voices a character, and the class watches the result together at the end of the week.

The teacher's MacPupils on school laptops or phones
05
  1. Keep it on the school networkLocal network mode needs no setup: pupils scan the QR code on the projector and type the password.
  2. Translate as homeworkThe class writes the subtitle in their own language, or one they're learning; the dub follows that text line by line.
  3. Hear yourself against the originalPupils hear the original line, record, and see whether their take fits the timing before sending.
  4. Fill the gaps with AICharacters nobody wanted can be generated, so the film is complete even in a small class.
  5. Premiere in the TheatrePlay the finished dub for the class, or one character at a time to talk about pronunciation.

A family makes a cartoon their children can follow

The children are too young for subtitles, and the show isn't dubbed into the family's language. A parent makes an Estonian or Irish dub at home, in the evening, with the family's own voices or AI voices.

A home MacFamily voices, imported
06
  1. Use the subtitles you haveA subtitle in your language, from the release or a fan translation, is all the script you need.
  2. Grandma reads the narratorRecord a minute of her talking on a phone and import it as a voice. Music is taken out and the best part is kept.
  3. Or record togetherParents and older children record their characters in the browser on the sofa, one line at a time.
  4. Watch it on the TVExport the MKV with the dub as default audio and the original kept, and play it on any player.
Grandparents too: a dub helps anyone who would rather listen than read, not only children.

A subtitle-only group gets cleaner subtitles and separated tracks

Not every group wants a dub. FanSUB's preparation alone fills in who says every line, finds the opening and ending, and makes a clean dialogue track and a no-dialogue track.

One MacNo session, no voices needed
07
  1. Assign lines fastThe video stops at the end of each line; press 1 to 9 for the speaker. Voice matching can suggest who it is.
  2. Let the checks find mistakesReview points out lines without a speaker, overlaps and speech running past the subtitle.
  3. Export for the groupAn ASS file with every Actor field filled in, multi-speaker lines kept as one event, plus a cast sheet for translators.
  4. Hand on the audioDialogue and no-dialogue as FLAC, or one MKA, for editors and karaoke timers.

A sound engineer polishes the mix

Home recordings sound like home recordings. The group's engineer shapes each character, makes robots sound like robots, and evens out ten different microphones.

The host's Mac, or a round tripYour own plug-ins and DAW
08
  1. An effect chain per characterPresets and Audio Unit plug-ins on the approved takes. Takes stay as recorded; effects are worked out at release and can be switched off.
  2. Compare before and afterPress B to flip, and override the chain for a single line.
  3. Round tripExport a character's track with timecode to another audio app and load it back when done.
  4. Fix single takes by handIn the take editor, under the scene’s video, with every word on the timeline: split between words, trim and move parts, change speed, pitch and level, with undo.
  5. Loudness and room soundRelease fandub evens out loudness between actors, matches the original episode and can add a little room sound so home recordings sit in the scene.

With DubAI GameDUB

A game translation team prepares a voiced game for a dub

A fan translation team has already put a game's text into Hungarian. The voices are still English. Before anyone records, they need every spoken line, who says it, and how long each one may last, out of a folder of numbered sound files and engine archives.

One MacGameDUB, with ffmpeg and vgmstream
09
  1. Scan the game's folderGameDUB recognises the engine (here Unity with Wwise) and says what it read and what to unpack, with which tool. After unpacking, Scan again keeps everything done so far.
  2. Get the voices outVoices inside Wwise banks are copied out with one button; Wwise, Ogg and Opus audio is converted to WAV so every line plays.
  3. Check the linesText and voice files are matched by key, speakers come from the table or the voice folders, and menus and item names are left out.
  4. Time and writeTranscription gives each line its speech start, end and pauses. The Script screen shows whether each translation fits that time and the game's length limit.
The game stays as it was. GameDUB only reads the game's folder; converted voices and sounds taken out of banks go into its own project folder.

Whichever group you are

You need one Mac with Apple silicon and macOS 26. Everyone else needs only a browser, and the AI voices are an optional download.
